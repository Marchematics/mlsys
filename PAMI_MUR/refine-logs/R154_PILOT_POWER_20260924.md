# R154 — The protocol as a decision procedure: how large a pilot is enough?

**Date:** 2026-09-24
**Artifact:** `results/derived/R154_pilot_power/pilot_power.json`
**Script:** `experiments/analyze_pilot_power.py`

## Why this experiment

Rounds 12–14 established three negatives that all point the same way: a
predictability probe cannot gate deployment (R140), structural headroom does not
predict routing benefit in either regime (R153), and the same contrast is
unresolved on two of six datasets (R144). What survives is the *realized share
measured under the deployment's own protocol*. The open question is operational:
**if a practitioner runs the protocol on a labelled pilot of the deployment, how
often does the pilot's verdict match the verdict on the full deployment?**

This turns the measurement protocol from a description into a decision procedure
and gives it measured operating characteristics.

## Design

Six datasets, ridge regime, 32 targets each, three seeds. For each dataset the
full-sample verdict is the paired target-level interval for R-MUR minus pool-all:
`AHEAD` if it excludes zero above, `BELOW` if below, `UNRESOLVED` otherwise.
Pilots of `n ∈ {4, 8, 12, 16, 24}` targets are drawn without replacement, 400
pilots per (dataset, size) — 2,400 pilots per size in total — and each pilot is
scored with the same rule on 1,500 bootstrap draws.

## Result

Full-sample verdicts: METR-LA, PEMS-BAY and PEMS07 `AHEAD`; PEMS03 and PEMS04
`UNRESOLVED`; PEMS08 `BELOW`.

| pilot size | decision agreement | sensitivity for `AHEAD` | specificity for `AHEAD` | pilot says `AHEAD` | pilot cost (ridge) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | .643 | .664 | .882 | .391 | ~0.4 min |
| 8 | .769 | .816 | .940 | .438 | ~0.7 min |
| 12 | .817 | .864 | .941 | .462 | ~1.1 min |
| 16 | .841 | .859 | .927 | .466 | ~1.4 min |
| **24** | **.959** | **.976** | **.957** | .510 | **~2.2 min** |

* **A pilot of 24 targets reproduces the full-sample verdict 96% of the time**,
  with 98% sensitivity and 96% specificity for the operationally critical
  `route` call. At 16 targets it is 84% / 86% / 93%.
* **The numbers are not a base-rate artefact.** Half the datasets are truly
  `AHEAD`, so a constant-`AHEAD` rule would score sensitivity 1.0 and
  specificity 0.0; the pilot's specificity stays at .88–.96 throughout.
* **Direction is easy, confidence is not.** A lax "sign agreement" measure
  (counting an unresolved pilot as non-contradicting) is ≈1.0 even at four
  targets, but strict three-way agreement is only .64 there. The pilot is good
  at telling which way the effect goes and needs ~24 targets to tell whether it
  is real.
* **Cost.** In the ridge regime a cell is 2–5 s, so a 24-target pilot is about
  two minutes. With the strong nonlinear backbone a cell is ~88 s, so the same
  pilot is ~35 minutes — still small against the cost of deploying a router that
  does not pay.

## The resulting procedure

1. Label a pilot of ~24 targets (or as many as budget allows; 16 gives ~85%).
2. Run the protocol unchanged on the pilot: screening, budget, objective, and
   both the router and the pool-everything baseline.
3. Compute the paired target-level interval for router minus pooling.
4. Route only if it excludes zero above; otherwise pool. Do **not** substitute a
   cheaper proxy: the predictability probe (R140) and the structural headroom
   (R153) are both non-predictive, and the cached screen's correlation with the
   true marginal is chance-level in the regime where this decision matters most.

## Caveats, stated rather than hidden

* The six datasets are all traffic forecasting. The operating characteristics
  are measured on six deployments, which is enough to show the procedure works
  but not enough to claim the exact rates transfer to another domain; the
  recommendation is the *procedure*, not the specific 96%.
* The pilot and the full deployment share targets drawn from the same sensor
  population, so this measures sampling stability rather than distribution
  shift. A pilot drawn from a different period would need its own check.
* The pilot verdict uses the same primary policy (`q=4`) as the full sample; a
  practitioner choosing the router configuration on the pilot would reintroduce
  the selection bias the protocol is meant to avoid.
