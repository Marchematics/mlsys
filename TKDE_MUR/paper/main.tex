\title{Identifiable Marginal Utility: Why Predictor Response Decides When Context Selection Works}
\author{Author details to be supplied}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Context selection assumes that a candidate's value can be estimated before the
outcome is observed. We study the missing premise: identifiability. We ask what
a router can know about the marginal reduction in loss produced by adding a
context to the current set, and how that knowledge changes with the fixed
predictor it routes into. The analysis gives an exact identity for Bregman
losses and two-sided bounds for general smooth losses, with explicit constants
for softmax cross-entropy, and shows that conditional on the state the
Bayes-optimal squared-loss utility is affine in the predictor's response. The
same identity yields a corollary with practical force: the identifiable part of
a candidate's value is the residual of the fixed predictor itself, so a better
predictor leaves less realizable opportunity even when the oracle opportunity
is unchanged. We turn this into a measurement protocol --- screen--truth
correlation, within-episode rank predictability, and a single label-free
redundancy statistic --- and into a mechanism-based selection rule that adapts
to it. Experiments span three regimes. Where utility is estimable (linear
subset expert, six traffic benchmarks) learned marginal-utility routing beats
fourteen competitors --- significantly ahead of the best of them on three
benchmarks, unresolved on two and below simple pooling on one --- while relevance
ranking and maximum marginal relevance are significantly worse than using no
context at all on four of the six, and the strongest competitor on every
benchmark is the trivial policy of including every candidate. Where it is not
estimable (a
subset-capable nonlinear expert) no method separates from simply including every
candidate, the optimal oracle budget is smaller than the protocol's, and
enlarging the library from sixteen to one hundred and twenty-eight candidates
raises the oracle opportunity by forty percent while driving the router to the
level of random selection. Where
candidates
contribute categorical evidence (demonstration selection on five image
benchmarks) four different scoring heads bracket the same ceiling. The
contribution is a diagnosis and a decision rule, not another ranking heuristic.
\end{abstract}
