\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Protocols and the three regimes}

Every experiment below fixes a budget of four contexts from a pool of sixteen
candidates unless stated otherwise, and holds the predictor frozen while
contexts are routed into it. Three regimes differ only in what the predictor
is, because \cref{cor:identifiability} predicts that this is what determines
whether selection can work.

\emph{Regime A, utility estimable.} The predictor is a subset-capable ridge
expert fitted per target, the protocol used by the controlled study and by the
six-dataset traffic benchmark. Candidate pools are built from training-split
correlations: half high-correlation neighbours, a third mid-correlation, the
rest random distractors. History length and horizon are both 12.

\emph{Regime B, utility not estimable.} The expert is replaced by a
subset-capable spatio-temporal transformer: per-node history embeddings,
learned node embeddings, time-of-day and day-of-week embeddings, three
transformer layers over the anchor token and the selected candidate tokens with
a key-padding mask, and a linear path from the anchor history
($499{,}722$ parameters). It is trained with subset dropout so that every
subset size is in distribution and unselected candidates are provably inert.
It is pretrained on the training split and then adapted per target on exactly
the episodes the ridge expert sees.

\emph{Regime C, categorical evidence.} Candidate contexts are labelled
demonstrations for a frozen in-context classifier: embeddings come from a
frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 encoder, the predictor is a transformer that reads the
query embedding and the embeddings and labels of the selected demonstrations,
and utility is the reduction of query-batch cross-entropy. Five public
benchmarks (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, SVHN, EuroSAT, DTD) are used with three seeds
and 1{,}602 test episodes each.

All comparisons are paired at the target or benchmark level with bootstrap or
$t$ intervals as noted, and every number is reproduced by the released scripts
with immutable raw runs.

\subsection{Regime A: utility is estimable}

\paragraph{State conditioning creates value.} In the controlled redundancy
study, structural headroom grows monotonically with the duplicate ratio
(\cref{tab:redundancy}). At zero redundancy the standalone and sequential
oracles coincide; at duplicate ratio $.75$ the gap reaches $.286$, and the
learned router exceeds the standalone oracle even though that oracle uses the
true standalone utilities. The gain therefore comes from re-evaluating
candidate value after the selected state changes, not from a better standalone
score.

Among the fourteen methods compared across the six benchmarks (the detailed
two-dataset matrix below reports eleven of them) is a DELIFT-style rule (ICLR 2025) that
combines submodular coverage with k-means representatives, implemented here as
greedy facility location over an over-segmented pool of cluster medoids. It is
significantly below pooling on all six datasets ($-.0196$ $[-.0241,-.0155]$ on
METR-LA, $-.0212$ $[-.0297,-.0128]$ on PEMS-BAY, and between $-.018$ and
$-.023$ elsewhere), so adding the most recent member of the submodular family
does not change the verdict: the strongest competitor is still the trivial
policy of using every candidate, and on every dataset the best non-trivial rule
is greedy mutual information.

\paragraph{Against eleven competitors, in detail.} We then ran the detailed competitor set on
the same cells: METR-LA and PEMS-BAY, 32 targets each, three seeds, 96 cells
per dataset (\cref{tab:ridge-matrix}). Two results stand out. First, \emph{the
default heuristics are significantly harmful on both datasets}: relevance
ranking is $.0165$ below the anchor-only predictor on METR-LA and $.0199$ below
on PEMS-BAY, maximum marginal relevance is $.0180$ and $.0249$ below, and on
METR-LA determinantal diversity, k-center, facility location, k-means
representatives, random subsets and even greedy mutual information are harmful
as well, each with a target-level interval entirely below zero. Selecting
correlated or diverse candidates by a proxy score injects noise that the
predictor propagates; the oracles show that a great deal of value exists
($+.133$ and $+.163$ standalone), so this is a selection failure rather than an
absence of signal. Second, learning the decision recovers that value and more:
the response-aware router reaches $+.0146$ and $+.0557$, roughly twice the
strongest non-selection baseline (pooling all sixteen candidates, $+.0080$ and
$+.0254$) and clearly above the response-free learned routers. The pattern generalises across the six benchmarks:
running the same competitors on all six datasets, with the frozen cells for the
learned policies and 32 targets and three seeds per dataset (96 cells each),
on five of the six datasets the strongest competitor is the trivial
pool-everything policy --- on PEMS-BAY the cached-only router ($.0284$) and the
static-utility model ($.0272$) edge past it ($.0254$) --- and the paired
comparison against the leader is what decides the outcome. Under
target-level paired intervals the
response-aware router is significantly ahead on three datasets --- METR-LA
$+.0067$ $[+.0041,+.0094]$, PEMS-BAY $+.0303$ $[+.0203,+.0427]$ and PEMS07
$+.0041$ $[+.0015,+.0070]$ --- is unresolved on two (PEMS03 $+.0013$
$[-.0004,+.0030]$ and PEMS04 $-.0008$ $[-.0050,+.0025]$, where the router
reaches $+.0357$ against pool-all $+.0365$) and is \emph{significantly below}
pooling on PEMS08 ($-.0026$ $[-.0042,-.0010]$, router $+.0333$ against pool-all
$+.0359$). We report the
loss rather than average it away: the method's advantage over the trivial
baseline is real but not universal, and on the two datasets where it fails the
competitor it fails to is pooling, not any of the specialised heuristics.
Relevance ranking is harmful
on four of the six and maximum marginal relevance on four; on the two dense-flow
datasets the proxies are weakly positive, so the harmful regime is common but
not universal.

\paragraph{What limits learned routing here.} Candidate-pool growth compresses
the decision margin: as $K$ grows from $4$ to $64$, absolute utility error
improves while the standard deviation of true marginals falls faster, so
normalised error rises from $.403$ to $.739$ and the positive top-two margin
falls from $.220$ to $.024$. Response features, by contrast, expand what is
observable: adding compact response summaries raises the Spearman correlation
with the true marginal from $.164$ to $.391$ and the positive-utility AUC from
$.649$ to $.834$, and reduces one-step regret from $.0736$ to $.0569$. Both
effects are consistent with \cref{sec:theory}: the decision is hard exactly
when the response is small relative to the utility spread.

\subsection{Applying the diagnostics}
\label{sec:diagnostics-applied}

\paragraph{Measurement 1: the cheap view is noise in regime B.} On METR-LA with
the nonlinear expert, the cached score's correlation with the true marginal is
indistinguishable from zero ($+.005$, $-.049$, $-.019$ at the first three
greedy steps), and the recall of the shortlist it forms equals chance: $.26$
and $.29$ at $q=4$ against a chance level of $.25$, and $.49$--$.53$ at $q=8$
against $.50$. Any router whose first stage is this screen cannot recover the
value it never received.

\paragraph{Measurement 2: the ordering is not recoverable.} We fitted ridge and
small multi-layer heads to the true marginals using every observable quantity
--- anchor history, candidate history, candidate identity, the response vector,
its magnitude and the base--response interaction --- on six (dataset, target)
cells with 1{,}500 training and 800 test episodes each. Every probe generalises
worse than the mean: out-of-sample $R^2$ averages $-.093$ (range $-.63$ to
$+.03$), the mean within-episode rank correlation with the true marginal is
$+.001$ and stays below $.12$ in eleven of twelve probes, while the in-sample
fit is $+.070$ and the label-permutation control is $-.005$. The control
establishes that the pipeline is sound; the conclusion is that the ordering is
not recoverable from observable features, not that the model is weak.

The probe is a diagnostic instrument, not a deployment rule, and we
deliberately stop short of turning it into one. Scaling the same probe up to
$2{,}000$ training and $1{,}200$ test episodes on three targets does not change
the verdict: out-of-sample $R^2$ stays within noise of zero for both a weak and
a strong expert ($-.004 \pm .019$ and $-.015 \pm .028$), and the probe's value
excess over its own per-cell random baseline is $+.048$ and $+.067$ of the
oracle against a standard deviation of $.064$ across the six cells. Since every $R^2$ we observe lies
below any threshold that would be useful, a rule of the form ``probe the
candidate expert, fall back to the whole pool if the probe is weak'' would fire
on every deployment; we do not propose it. What the protocol supports is a
ranking of \emph{geometries} (Measurements 1 and 3), not a per-cell go/no-go
test, and the executable test of identifiability remains the routing protocol
itself.

\paragraph{Measurement 3: novelty predicts which policy works.} Building three
pool geometries per dataset --- head of the pool drawn from the most
correlated, the middle, or the least correlated sensors --- the relevance gain
rises monotonically as candidates become less redundant with the anchor
(\cref{tab:pool-modes}), and the sign flip replicates on the second dataset
with a larger swing ($-.033 \to +.016$ on PEMS-BAY).

Across the six pool geometries novelty correlates $+.80$ with the relevance
gain, and the two regimes separate without overlap: every harmful cell has
novelty $\le .46$, every helpful cell $\ge .60$. The statistic does not, however, transfer across
datasets as a constant: over the six standard pools the correlation with the
relevance gain is $-.33$. Redundancy with the anchor is the mechanism that
operates when the pool is manipulated inside one dataset; across datasets other
factors dominate. That is precisely why the protocol measures the statistic per
deployment rather than transferring it, and it is a negative result we report
rather than a caveat we hide. This is also why the
demonstration family behaves in the opposite way: a demonstration contributes a
label the anchor lacks, so its novelty is high by construction even though its
embedding is close to the query.

\paragraph{The prediction tested directly.} \Cref{cor:identifiability} says
that the identifiable term is the fixed predictor's own residual, so a stronger
predictor should leave less realizable value while the structural opportunity
persists. We built a ladder of experts on identical cells --- eight targets,
seed 101, the same pools and episodes --- using three spatio-temporal
transformers of increasing capacity, a masked-mean MLP, and the per-target ridge
expert as an out-of-family contrast. Strength is the anchor-only mean squared
error on held-out windows; realizable value is the learned router's share of the
standalone-oracle gain.

Within the neural family the prediction holds as a monotone dose--response: the
structural opportunity is flat ($+.084$ to $+.100$ standalone, $+.096$ to
$+.120$ sequential) while the realized share falls by a factor of $4.3$ and the
learned gain by a factor of $3.0$ as anchor-only error falls from $.372$ to
$.356$. Across the four neural rungs anchor-only error orders realized share
perfectly (Spearman $+1.0$) and the fitted slope is $10.5$ realized-share points
per unit anchor MSE, so a $1\%$ anchor improvement ($.0036$ in MSE) corresponds
to about $+.038$ share. A $4\%$ improvement in the expert removes three quarters
of the value that routing can realise.

The ridge rung is the informative exception. It has the worst anchor-only error
($.3782$) yet the largest structural opportunity ($+.1535$, because its residual
is highly systematic) and the largest absolute learned gain ($+.0313$); its
realized share is nonetheless below two of the transformers because the
denominator is larger. Including it, the rank correlation between anchor error
and realized share drops and the fitted slope halves to $4.9$. The residual
magnitude is thus a sufficient statistic for realizable value only \emph{within}
a fixed hypothesis class: across families the inductive bias changes which
candidates are jointly improvable, so accuracy and residual geometry decouple.

This is also where the diagnostic protocol reaches its limit, and we report the
limit rather than the hoped-for result. If residual predictability could be read
off cheaply, it would give a deployment rule --- probe the candidate expert, and
decline to select when the probe is weak. We tested that rule on the two ladder
extremes at three targets with $2{,}000$ training and $1{,}200$ test episodes
each. The probe's out-of-sample $R^2$ for the marginal is indistinguishable from
zero for both extremes ($-.004 \pm .019$ for the weak rung, $-.015 \pm .028$ for
the strong one), and its value statistic has to be read against a per-cell
random baseline that itself ranges from $.115$ to $.294$ of the oracle; excess
over random is $+.048$ and $+.067$ of the oracle with a per-cell spread of
$.09$, against a ladder effect of $.205$ on the same two rungs. A statistic
whose excess is a quarter of the effect it should explain, and whose noise
exceeds that excess, cannot gate deployment, and at these sample sizes the
threshold rule degenerates to always falling back to the whole pool. The
identifiability of a candidate must therefore be measured by running the routing
protocol itself --- screening, budget, and objective included --- not by an
auxiliary probe, however cheap. Measurement~2 above reports the same conclusion
from the opposite direction: the probe is a diagnostic instrument, not a gate.

The same test in the regime where routing \emph{does} work makes the
negative general. Over the six-dataset ridge protocol, 32 targets each
(192 targets in total), the within-dataset correlation between a target's
structural headroom and the router's advantage over pooling is $+.040$
($p=.58$); per dataset it ranges from $-.235$ to $+.310$ with no consistent
sign. Across datasets the mean headroom and the mean advantage are more alike
($r=+.60$, $p=.21$, $n=6$) but the ordering is broken by the most informative
case: PEMS03 has the \emph{highest} mean headroom of the six ($.119$) and an
advantage of $+.0013$, while PEMS-BAY has similar headroom ($.115$) and the
largest advantage ($+.0303$). Structural headroom is therefore a necessary
condition --- a router cannot capture what the oracle cannot reach --- and not
a usable predictor of routing benefit in either regime. What does discriminate
is the \emph{realised} share measured under the deployment's own protocol, and
that is what the diagnostic protocol of \cref{sec:diagnostics} should be read
as measuring.

A corollary worth stating, since it bears on how the six-dataset result should
be read: the datasets differ by effect size rather than by statistical power.
The per-target standard error of the router-versus-pooling difference is
comparable across benchmarks ($.0008$ to $.0059$), while the effect itself
ranges from $t=+5.1$ on PEMS-BAY (94\% of targets favour routing) through
$t=+1.5$ on PEMS03 (69\%) to $t=-3.1$ on PEMS08 (38\%). PEMS03 is a genuinely
small effect, not an under-powered one: resolving it at its observed size would
need roughly three times as many targets.

\subsection{Regime B: utility is not estimable}

\Cref{tab:strong-backbone} reports the strong-backbone protocol on METR-LA and
PEMS-BAY with 16 fixed targets and three seeds. Structural headroom is
preserved --- the sequential oracle gains $.105$ and $.104$ over the
anchor-only predictor, the standalone oracle $.090$ and $.088$ --- so context
selection still matters in principle. Yet no method separates from pooling
every candidate. The response-aware routers reach $.0097$ and $.0107$ (frozen and
theory-shaped heads) against pool-all $.0133$, and on PEMS-BAY every learned
router, including ours, is below plain random selection ($.0163$). Greedy
mutual information ties pool-all ($.0134$ against $.0133$).

The ordering inside this regime is not resolved, and we state it as a tie
rather than a loss. Under target-level pairing the difference between the
response-aware router and pool-all is $-.0036$ with an interval of
$[-.0110,+.0067]$ over the $16$ METR-LA targets (three seeds per target), and on
the $8$-target subsample used for the adaptation sweep below the same difference
is $+.0045$ $[-.0036,+.0130]$: the point estimate changes sign with the target
sample and neither interval excludes zero. What regime~B supports is therefore the weaker but sufficient claim that
\emph{no method separates from full pooling}, while all of them remain far
below the oracle ($.085$--$.100$). The failure is one of realisability, not of
ranking.

The two datasets resolve the comparison differently, and the paired tests say
which. On PEMS-BAY pooling is significantly above \emph{every} alternative ---
response-free or response-aware --- and above even random selection: the paired
difference is $-.0094$ $[-.0135,-.0053]$ for the frozen response-aware head and
$-.0091$ $[-.0126,-.0057]$ for the cached-only router. On METR-LA, using the
same target-level pairing throughout, pooling is
significantly above every response-free rule (relevance $-.0062$

($-.0036$ $[-.0110,+.0067]$ and $-.0025$ $[-.0093,+.0075]$). So the response
signal is the only thing that keeps a router in contact with pooling once the
predictor is strong; whether it closes the gap is dataset-dependent and here
unresolved. Both sit an order of magnitude below the oracle.

\paragraph{The budget is the wrong lever.} \Cref{fig:budget-gap} (left) shows
why neither better heuristics nor budget tuning closes the gap. With the true
marginals the best budget is \emph{three} contexts ($+.105$), and every
additional context reduces the gain: the oracle is not monotone in the budget.
Without identifiability the picture inverts --- random $k$-subsets improve
monotonically with $k$ --- so the best deployable policy in the entire matrix is
to include all sixteen candidates ($+.026$ on this reduced target set). The two
curves move in opposite directions through the protocol's budget of four, and
the vertical distance between them at $k=4$ is the identifiability gap.

\paragraph{Neither is the size of the library.} The most natural escape from
the tie is that the pool is simply too small: with a realistic library of
hundreds of candidates, pooling all of them should be infeasible and selection
should win. \Cref{tab:pool-size} tests this by holding the budget at four and
growing the pool from $16$ to $128$ candidates on the same $16$ cells.

The premise of the objection holds: the opportunity really does grow, from
$+.0851$ to $+.1196$, and the paired growth of the identifiability gap is
resolved at every step ($+.0181$ $[+.0126,+.0239]$, then $+.0257$
$[+.0197,+.0315]$, then $+.0446$ $[+.0376,+.0514]$ over the full range,
saturating between $64$ and $128$). The conclusion is the opposite of the
objection. The router captures \emph{less}, falling from $+.0076$ to $-.0025$;
at $32$ and $64$ candidates it is significantly \emph{below} pooling
($-.0097$ $[-.0159,-.0022]$ and $-.0114$ $[-.0192,-.0018]$); and beyond $16$
candidates it is indistinguishable from picking four at random (paired
$-.0018$, $-.0052$, $-.0019$). Enlarging the shortlist does not repair it
($q{=}8$ minus $q{=}4$ is $+.0011$ at $16$ and $-.0014$, $-.0016$ at $64$ and
$128$), which rules out the simple fix of buying more shortlist slots, though
it does not by itself separate a noisy screen from an unidentifiable marginal.
Pooling, meanwhile, is flat in pool size, so the decline is not caused by
pooling degrading. The binding constraint is identifiability, not the supply of
candidates, and retrieving from a larger library makes the routing problem
harder rather than easier.

The theory's own limit case was tested next, and it fails. As the anchor
absorbs its residual the coefficient $g_A$ of \cref{eq:prop-suff} goes to zero, so
the score collapses to the observable curvature $-\|\dAj\|^2$ and the optimal
action should be to select the candidates that perturb the predictor
\emph{least}. That prediction is sharp and falsifiable, and it is refuted: the
least-perturbing policy gains $+.0026$ on METR-LA and $+.0037$ on PEMS-BAY
against pooling's $+.0133$ and $+.0224$, i.e.\ it is significantly \emph{below}
pooling on both ($-.0106$ $[-.0163,-.0051]$ and $-.0188$ $[-.0227,-.0150]$) and
ranks thirteenth and sixteenth of the eighteen non-oracle policies. The sign of
the theory's limit is visible --- it is the best of the six response-geometry
rules on METR-LA --- but a score that only avoids harm cannot create value: the
candidates that barely move the predictor are also the ones that contribute
least. The regime-B boundary therefore lies in the absence of the interaction
term $\langle g_A,\dAj\rangle$, not in how the score is written.

The remaining alternative --- that the \emph{screen} is at fault, since its
correlation with the true marginal is chance-level in this regime --- can be
removed directly. Setting $q=K=16$ so that the response head sees every
candidate changes the result by $+.0012$ $[-.0023,+.0044]$: $+.0088$ against
$+.0076$ for $q=4$, still not above pooling and still not above random four.
The screen is a symptom of the identification failure, not its cause, and the
two-stage design is justified independently --- at this pool size the shortlist
is free in value terms while costing $20$ expert rows per episode rather than
$64$.

The direction of the greedy search is not the problem: running selection
backwards, from the full pool by elimination, matches forward greedy to within
$+.0002$ ($[-.00003,+.0005]$, backward better on 18 of 24 cells), so the
utility landscape behaves submodularly for search purposes. Nor is robustness
to bad candidates the explanation: rebuilding the pools from the least
correlated sensors leaves pooling everything with the highest point estimate
($+.0040$) and the learned routers below it ($+.0013$), against oracle gains of
$+.066$ and $+.082$. An expert trained with subset dropout simply tolerates
irrelevant contexts, which removes the leverage selection would need. Again the
ordering is unresolved --- R-MUR against pooling is $-.0027$
$[-.0072,+.0020]$ --- and it is also unstable in sign: on an independently
constructed anticorrelated pool, relevance is significantly above pooling
($+.0051$ $[+.0025,+.0077]$). What is stable is the size of the residual gap to
the oracle.

\paragraph{Where a method change could still pay.} Every remaining loss in this
regime has one shape: the router wins on average while losing on a substantial
minority of episodes, and on PEMS08 it loses on average. The per-episode gains
all six deployments record put a ceiling on what any episode-level confidence
gate could recover: taking the better of routing and pooling episode by episode
would add $+.0148$ to $+.0778$ over pooling, three to seven times the router's
own advantage, and would convert the PEMS08 loss into a gain. The router loses
on $39$--$52\%$ of episodes, so the loss is a near-even split rather than a few
outliers a safety rule could excise. We then tested whether that headroom is reachable, and it is not.
Four signals available at decision time were recorded for every episode --- the
cached screen's top-two margin, the utility model's top-two margin, its top
score, and the prediction-change norm of the candidate it picked --- and each was
scored for predicting whether the router beats pooling on that episode. All
twelve dataset-by-signal AUCs lie in $[.481,.521]$: the router's own confidence
carries no information about when it is wrong, and the best held-out gate adds
at most $+.0030$ over pooling against a ceiling of $.013$ to $.048$. This is what
\cref{cor:identifiability} predicts rather than a defect of this router --- the
identifiable term is the anchor's residual, a strong anchor has absorbed its
systematic part, and what remains is noise, which no function of the same
absorbed signal can anticipate. The remaining loss is therefore not a tuning,
head-design or gating problem: the estimation error is present and
unobservable.

\subsection{The adaptive rule}

The third measurement converts into the policy of \cref{eq:adaptive-rule}. With
the threshold chosen on half of the targets and then held fixed, the rule
reaches $+.0081\;[+.0017,+.0138]$ on METR-LA and $+.0129\;[-.0066,+.0329]$ on
PEMS-BAY, against $-.0006$ and $-.0020$ for fixed relevance and $+.0066$ and
$+.0098$ for always pooling everything; it captures $87\%$ and $67\%$ of an
oracle that picks the better of the two policies per cell. The selected
threshold is stable across splits on METR-LA ($[.45,.55]$ in $198$ of $200$)
but not on PEMS-BAY, where it concentrates at the grid minimum ($.40$ in $91$
of $200$ splits); it should be fitted per deployment rather than transferred. The benefit is resolved on METR-LA and unresolved on PEMS-BAY, and on
neither dataset does the rule separate from always pooling ($+.0015$ and
$+.0031$): what it buys is escape from a harmful default, not superiority over
the trivial policy. A training-free practitioner should therefore adapt the
retrieval rule
to pool geometry rather than apply it unconditionally; a practitioner who can
learn the decision should use marginal-utility routing, which is roughly twice
as good on the standard pools.

\subsection{Regime C: categorical evidence}

In demonstration selection the predictor consumes labelled examples, so the
mechanism of \cref{sec:theory} applies with a different information type. The
predictor is genuinely in-context: mean accuracy rises from $.966$ to $.983$ on
CIFAR-10, $.812$ to $.997$ on CIFAR-100, $.693$ to $.996$ on SVHN, $.946$ to
$.986$ on EuroSAT and $.728$ to $.921$ on DTD when four relevant demonstrations
are added, and the farthest demonstrations are worse than random ones.

The frozen head passes the per-benchmark criterion on three of five benchmarks
and fails on CIFAR-100 and DTD, where a nearest-neighbour relevance rule is
already within a fraction of a percent of the oracle. The family also exposes
a boundary condition: the sequential and standalone oracles coincide to four
decimals ($H_{\mathrm{state}}\le 5\times10^{-4}$), because the candidate pools
contain no redundant demonstrations, so selecting the best standalone
demonstrations is already optimal.

\paragraph{Four heads, one ceiling.} We iterated on the scoring head to test
whether the failure is one of functional form. A redundancy-structured variant
of the pools was built first (four clusters of three near-duplicate
demonstrations); measured redundancy was real --- a second member of a cluster
retains only $6.7$--$42\%$ of the first member's marginal --- yet the
sequential oracle still does not meaningfully exceed the standalone oracle,
because utility in this family is label-driven: replacing candidate
\emph{features} by noise costs only about $18\%$ of the first-member marginal,
while equalising \emph{labels} flips every marginal negative. For that
predictor the utility is nearly a function of the label multiset of the
selected set, so no pool geometry can make it state-conditioned.

Replacing the predictor with a query-comparative one (a learned-metric
prototype in which a demonstration can only help through its image content)
closes that shortcut: the second-member marginal falls to $0.12$--$0.78\%$ of
the first, and pooled state-conditioned headroom becomes genuinely positive
($+.0126\;[+.0019,+.0239]$). In that setting the theory-shaped bilinear head of
\cref{eq:bilinear-head} ranks the true marginal better than the scalar head on
four of five benchmarks (mean within-state Spearman $.375$ against $.156$;
top-one agreement $.67$ against $.08$ on CIFAR-100) and gives the largest pooled
forced-budget contrast against Standalone Utility of the three heads
($+.0605\;[-.0036,+.1343]$, three of five per-benchmark intervals above zero).
The residual failure has a precise cause: the association between response
magnitude and marginal utility changes sign across benchmarks ($+.257$ on
CIFAR-10, $-.590$ on CIFAR-100) while the identity's curvature term has a fixed
sign. Releasing that constraint with a learned, signed coefficient confirms the
diagnosis --- the coefficient moves from its $-.25$ initialisation to $+.14$,
$+.13$, $+.11$ and $+.08$ on four of five benchmarks ($p<10^{-6}$ against both
$-.25$ and $0$) --- and still does not improve the gate (pooled
$+.0528\;[-.0081,+.1236]$, two of five per-benchmark passes), because the extra
unit re-fits the same ordering. Four head variants --- scalar,
curvature-corrected, bilinear and signed bilinear --- therefore bracket the same
result, which locates the ceiling in what the router can observe rather than in
how the score is parameterised.

\subsection{Compute}

Each decision in regime B spends $B(1+q)$ expert evaluations: $20$ per episode
at $q=4$ and $36$ at $q=8$, against $64$ for reranking the full pool and $17$
for the standalone oracle. Measured at deployment batch size on the same cell,
the response-aware stage costs $.74$, $1.19$, $1.59$ and $2.65$ milliseconds
per episode for $q=2,4,8,16$, against $1.70$ milliseconds for the sequential
oracle and $.03$ milliseconds for cached screening alone
($0$ expert evaluations), and it raises peak device memory from $90$ to
$173$ megabytes. The shortlist therefore retains its intended efficiency; in
regime B the trend across shortlist sizes ($q=4$: $.0097$, $q=8$: $.0108$) also
shows that the screen, not the response stage, is the limiting component.
