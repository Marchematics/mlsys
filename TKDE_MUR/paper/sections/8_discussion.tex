\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Structural opportunity is not realizable opportunity}

The three regimes separate two quantities that are easy to conflate.
\emph{Structural} headroom is a property of the predictor and the candidate
pool: it is the gap between the sequential and the standalone oracle, and it is
positive in every dataset and target we measured, including the strong-backbone
regime. \emph{Realizable} headroom is a property of what the router can observe.
Regime A has both: the ridge expert leaves a systematic residual, the learned
router converts a double-digit share of the structural opportunity into gain,
and it beats every competitor. Regime B has the first and not the second: the
stronger expert has already absorbed the systematic part of its own residual,
so the term that \cref{cor:identifiability} leaves to learn is close to noise.
Nothing in the matrix recovers the oracle value, and the practical consequence
is counter-intuitive --- with a strong predictor the best deployable policy is
to use every context, and the optimal budget shrinks rather than grows. Two
controlled sweeps sharpen that statement into a mechanism rather than a
boundary. Adapting the predictor is the operation that destroys realizable
value: two epochs of fine-tuning remove about five sixths of the share of the
oracle the router can realise while the structural opportunity is unchanged,
and twenty-eight further epochs add $1.2\%$ of accuracy and no identifiable
value. Enlarging the candidate library does the same from the other side: the
oracle opportunity rises by forty percent from sixteen to one hundred and
twenty-eight candidates while the router falls to the level of random
selection. Realizable headroom is therefore not a fixed property of a
deployment that one can measure once; it is consumed by adaptation and diluted
by scale, and both are choices.

\subsection{What the measurements are for}

The three diagnostics of \cref{sec:diagnostics} are the paper's most transferable
artefact, because they answer a question that precedes method choice.
Measurement 1 is cheap and decisive: if the cached view does not correlate with
the true marginal, no reranking stage can help. Measurement 2 settles whether
the ordering is recoverable at all, and it includes its own controls so that a
negative result is informative rather than a symptom of a weak model.
Measurement 3 turns the answer into a policy: candidate novelty separates the
regime in which retrieval helps from the regime in which it hurts, with no
label cost. In regime B all three fail together, and they fail in the direction
the theory predicts.

\subsection{Why the default toolkit is harmful rather than merely weak}

In regime A every training-free heuristic we tested is worse than using no
auxiliary context. The mechanism is visible in the pool sweep: when the head of
the pool is the most correlated with the anchor, a candidate adds little that
the anchor does not already contain, while the predictor's linear combination
of redundant inputs amplifies noise. Retrieval and diversity proxies are
therefore not merely uninformative in this regime; they are systematically
misleading, and the same proxies become helpful as soon as candidates carry
independent information. The adaptive rule of \cref{eq:adaptive-rule} is the
practical response, and it is deliberately conservative: it does not try to beat
learned routing, only to stop a default from doing harm.

\subsection{Why four scoring heads hit the same ceiling}

Regime C is the cleanest evidence that the limit is observational rather than
parametric. The bilinear head, which hard-codes the structure implied by
\cref{prop:suff}, ranks the marginal substantially better than a generic scalar
head; the signed variant confirms the diagnosis that the identity's fixed
curvature sign was binding, with a learned coefficient that is positive on four
of five benchmarks; and yet neither head changes the verdict. What limits
performance is the information in the router's observation, not the capacity of
the function fitted to it. This is the same conclusion the regime-B diagnostics
reach by a different route, and it is the reason the paper's contribution is a
diagnosis and a decision rule rather than another ranking heuristic.

\subsection{Limitations}

The bounds are conditional on the state and require the loss gradient at the
current prediction; at decision time the outcome is unobserved, so the router
estimates the expectation of the first-order term from training episodes. The
theory therefore explains the feature design and the second-order error of a
response-based surrogate; it is not a label-free guarantee. Regime B uses two
datasets and sixteen targets, and the pooled advantage of our method over the
response-free routers is no longer statistically resolved there --- a negative
result we report rather than tune away. Regime C's headroom is small
($+.0126$ pooled), so all heads compete inside a narrow band. Finally, the
strong-backbone expert is one architecture; whether the identifiability limit
softens for predictors with different inductive biases is an open question, and
the diagnostics of \cref{sec:diagnostics} are the tool with which to answer it
for any particular pair of predictor and pool.
