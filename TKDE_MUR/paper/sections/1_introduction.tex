\section{Introduction}

Predictive systems increasingly operate with more auxiliary information than
they should use indiscriminately: neighbouring sensors in a network, retrieved
documents for a language model, demonstrations for an in-context classifier, or
feature blocks for a tabular predictor. The central decision is not simply
which candidate is relevant in isolation, but which candidate will improve the
prediction that has already been formed from the contexts selected so far.

We study that decision through \emph{state-conditioned marginal utility}. For
a selected set $A$ and a remaining candidate $j$, the marginal is the expected
reduction in task loss produced by adding $j$ to $A$. This definition separates
three questions that are often conflated. The first is structural: does
re-evaluating candidates after each addition create value over a fixed ranking?
The second is statistical: is the resulting candidate ordering recoverable from
quantities available before the target outcome is known? The third is
algorithmic: if it is recoverable, how should the selector spend predictor
evaluations to identify the next useful context?

The key observable is the predictor's \emph{response},
$d_{A,j}=f_{A\cup\{j\}}-f_A$. We show that this is not merely a convenient
feature. For general smooth losses, marginal utility admits a response
expansion with a controlled second-order remainder. For Bregman losses the
relation becomes an exact identity, and for softmax cross-entropy the curvature
constant is explicit. Under squared loss, conditioning on the state makes the
Bayes-optimal utility affine in the response plus a known quadratic penalty.
The only unknown coefficient is the residual of the fixed predictor itself.
This yields a concrete prediction: when model adaptation removes systematic
residual structure, structural oracle opportunity may remain while the part of
that opportunity that can be identified before observing the outcome shrinks.

The theory motivates a response-aware selector. A cached model first screens
the candidate pool, after which only a shortlist is probed through the fixed
predictor. A response-aware head reranks the retained candidates by estimated
marginal utility. We evaluate both a generic head and a theory-shaped bilinear
head that represents the response--residual interaction explicitly. The same
analysis also motivates diagnostics for whether routing is worth attempting at
all: shortlist signal, within-episode rank predictability from observable
features, and candidate novelty as a label-free description of pool geometry.

The resulting experiments are deliberately organised around regimes rather
than around one benchmark table. In an identifiable traffic regime with a
linear subset expert, response-aware marginal routing is the strongest
deployable selector in the detailed comparisons against content, fixed-set,
utility and learned alternatives. With stronger subset-capable predictors, the
oracle opportunity remains substantial but candidate-level utility becomes
statistically indistinguishable from noise under the available observations;
enlarging the candidate library increases the oracle opportunity while reducing
the fraction that routing can realise. A controlled pool-geometry sweep then
shows that the sign of relevance depends on novelty, which yields a label-free
adaptive rule. Finally, demonstration selection on five image benchmarks
provides a second task family in which ranking and stopping can be separated
experimentally.

These results support four contributions. We formulate state-conditioned
marginal utility as the common decision object for budgeted context selection.
We derive a general response--utility theory, including an exact Bregman
identity, a softmax-cross-entropy bound and a response-sufficiency result. We
develop response-aware and theory-shaped selectors together with measurable
identifiability diagnostics. We then use a broad experimental programme ---
recent selector baselines, nonlinear backbones, candidate-library scaling,
alternative scoring heads, a second task family, compute measurements and
deployment pilots --- to connect strong positive results to the mechanism that
determines when those results are attainable.

A practical consequence is that stronger prediction and easier selection are
not the same objective. An adapted predictor can absorb the systematic residual
that gives a router its signal; a larger candidate library can increase the
oracle opportunity while making the ordering harder to identify; and a
retrieval heuristic can change sign as the candidate pool becomes more or less
novel relative to the anchor. Context selection should therefore be evaluated
as a joint property of the predictor, the candidate pool and the information
available to the selector, not as a fixed ranking problem.
