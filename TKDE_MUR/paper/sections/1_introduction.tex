\section{Introduction}

Prediction systems increasingly have more auxiliary information available than
they can afford to use: neighbouring sensors in a sensor network, retrieved
documents for a language model, labelled examples for an in-context classifier,
candidate feature blocks in a tabular pipeline. Choosing which of them to feed
a fixed predictor is usually framed as a ranking problem. Candidates are scored
--- by relevance, by diversity, by a learned utility model --- and the top few
are selected.

Ranking presumes that a candidate's value can be estimated \emph{before} the
outcome is observed. This paper studies that premise. We call a candidate's
value \emph{identifiable} at a decision point when it can be recovered, up to
useful accuracy, from information the router actually has: the candidate
descriptors, the current state, and the frozen predictor's behaviour. Our
starting point is a decomposition that makes identifiability concrete. For a
smooth loss, the marginal value of adding candidate $j$ to the selected set $A$
satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \marginal(j\mid A)
 =
 -\E\!\left[\grad\loss(f_A,y)^\top \dAj\right]
 +O\!\left(\E\|\dAj\|^2\right)
 -\lambda c_j ,
 \label{eq:teaser}
\end{equation}
where $\dAj=f_{A\cup\{j\}}-f_A$ is the predictor's \emph{response} to the
candidate. The leading term is an inner product between the response and the
loss gradient at the current prediction. The response is observable as soon as
the predictor is probed; the gradient is not, because it depends on the
outcome.

Two consequences organise the paper. First, the response is the right thing to
observe: for Bregman losses the decomposition is an exact identity rather than
an expansion, for softmax cross-entropy the remainder has a known constant, and
for squared loss the conditional expectation is affine in the response --- so
response features are not a heuristic but the sufficient statistic implied by
the loss. Second, the part that must be learned is the predictor's own
\emph{residual}, which means that improving the predictor removes exactly the
signal the router needs. A strong predictor is therefore not merely a harder
setting for context selection; it is a setting in which the selection problem
loses its identifiable content, even though the oracle value is unchanged.

We make this measurable and actionable.

\paragraph{Theory.} We give four results. A second-order expansion with a
two-sided bound for losses with Lipschitz gradients; an exact identity for
Bregman losses that contains the familiar squared-loss expression as a special
case; explicit constants for softmax cross-entropy, where the Hessian is
positive semidefinite with spectral norm at most $1/2$; and a
response-sufficiency statement showing that conditional on the state the
Bayes-optimal utility is affine in the response. Together they say that
predictor response carries information about marginal utility for any smooth
loss, and they say precisely where that information lives.

\paragraph{Measurement.} We turn the theory into a protocol that can be run
before committing to a selection method: measure how well the cheap cached view
correlates with the true marginal, measure within-episode rank predictability
from every observable feature, and measure one label-free pool statistic ---
candidate novelty, the share of a candidate's variance the anchor does not
already explain. Across three regimes these measurements predict, in advance,
whether selection will help at all.

\paragraph{Decision rule.} The mechanism yields a rule rather than a new
heuristic. When candidate novelty is high, retrieval-style ranking is the right
policy; when it is low, the best deployable policy is to include everything,
because a redundant pool makes proxy scores actively harmful. We give a
label-free adaptive rule with a threshold selected on held-out targets, and we
show it converts a harmful default into a beneficial one on two datasets. For
settings where the decision can be learned, we describe the response-aware
router used throughout, and we show that its head should be shaped by the
identity rather than discovered from data.

\paragraph{Evidence.} The empirical study has three regimes, chosen so that
identifiability differs while everything else is held fixed.
\begin{itemize}
 \item \emph{Utility is estimable.} With a linear subset expert on six traffic
 benchmarks, learned marginal-utility routing beats fourteen competitors on both
 datasets examined in detail --- significantly ahead of the best competitor on
 three of six benchmarks, unresolved on two and below simple pooling on one ---
 roughly
 doubling the strongest non-selection
 baseline, while relevance ranking, maximum marginal relevance, determinantal
 diversity, k-center, facility location, k-means representatives and random
 selection are \emph{significantly worse than using no auxiliary context at
 all}.
 \item \emph{Utility is not estimable.} Replacing the expert with a
 subset-capable nonlinear predictor, with the protocol otherwise unchanged,
 removes the advantage: no method beats including every candidate, and the
 oracle's optimal budget is \emph{smaller} than the protocol's, so the loss
 comes from the budget constraint rather than from bad ranking.
 \item \emph{Candidates contribute categorical evidence.} In demonstration
 selection for a frozen in-context classifier, four different scoring heads ---
 scalar, curvature-corrected, bilinear and signed bilinear --- bracket the same
 ceiling, which locates the limit in what can be observed rather than in how the
 score is parameterised.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper follows those three questions: why response
determines utility (\cref{sec:theory}), when utility is identifiable
(\cref{sec:diagnostics}), and how to select once identifiability is known
(\cref{sec:adaptive}). \Cref{sec:experiments} reports the three regimes and
\cref{sec:discussion} states the boundary and its practical consequences.

\paragraph{Scope and companion manuscripts.} This paper studies
identifiability: what determines whether marginal utility can be estimated at
all, and what follows for practice when it cannot. The method itself, its
implementation and its engineering evaluation are reported in a companion
manuscript, which also carries the multi-benchmark and second-task-family
evidence; the present paper reuses that protocol only as an instrument and does
not restate it. Neither paper depends on the other for its claims: the
identifiability results here are stated and proved from the utility definition,
and the experiments are self-contained.
